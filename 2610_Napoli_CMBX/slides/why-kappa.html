CMB secondaries: anchors with their own systematics

What the CMB picks up on its way
  • lensing κ: all matter, $z \approx 0.5\text{–}5$
  • CIB: dusty star formation, $z \approx 1\text{–}4$
  • tSZ: hot gas in haloes, $z \lesssim 1.5$
  • no shape noise, IA or photo-z on the CMB side

What is plotted

Two panels on one redshift axis. Top: where each signal comes from, the kernels that play the role of an n(z). Bottom: lensing kernels $W(z)$, the lensing efficiency per unit redshift.

Top, first step: the n(z) of the six Euclid DR1 weak-lensing source bins, each normalised to unit area and drawn with the analysis's Hann smoothing (width 0.05). Second step: the tSZ and CIB kernels, each scaled to its own peak because their units differ from an n(z) and from each other.

Bottom, first step: the lensing kernel of each source bin, from the official tomographic n(z) product (DpdBinMeanRedshift, PHZ-weighted, South release, May 2026; mean redshifts 0.45 to 1.73). Second step: CMB lensing, the same lensing efficiency with a single source plane at last scattering, $z_* \approx 1100.$ The shear kernels and CMB lensing are in the same units, so their heights compare: CMB lensing peaks at $z \approx 1.9$, about 2.4 times the peak of the highest source bin's kernel, and stays above half its peak from $z \approx 0.4$ to beyond $z = 3.$ The shear kernels peak below $z \approx 0.7$ and are gone by $z \approx 2.5.$

The CMB secondaries

The cosmic infrared background at 545 GHz: the bias-weighted emissivity of the Shang et al. (2012) halo model at CCL's default parameters, peaking at $z \approx 2.6$ and above half its peak from $z \approx 1.2$ to beyond $z = 3$ (to $z \approx 4.5$).

The thermal Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect: the bias-weighted mean electron pressure in a generalised-NFW halo model, peaking at $z \approx 0.3$ and above half its peak below $z \approx 1.4.$ This is not a cluster selection function: a single cluster's SZ decrement does not dim with redshift, which is why SZ cluster catalogues are close to mass-limited out to high redshift. But the tSZ signal per unit redshift, which is what a cross-correlation sees, follows the abundance of massive, hot haloes, and that falls steeply beyond $z \sim 1$; most of the y signal comes from $z \lesssim 1.5.$

Bias-weighted kernels are what a cross-correlation sees on linear scales. The CIB and tSZ shapes depend on the model, the CIB peak most of all; they show where each signal comes from, not a measurement.

Why it matters

Lensing and the CIB weight structure behind and across our source bins, where the shear kernels have little weight; the tSZ overlaps the low-redshift bins and traces hot gas rather than all matter. All three are measured from CMB maps, with no shape noise, intrinsic alignments or photometric redshifts on that side; their noise and foregrounds are of another kind, independent of the shear catalogue. A cross-spectrum still inherits the galaxy side's systematics: $\gamma\kappa$ carries the sources' multiplicative bias, n(z) and an intrinsic-alignment term, $\delta\kappa$ the galaxy bias.
