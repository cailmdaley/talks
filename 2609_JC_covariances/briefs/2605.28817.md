# Hadzhiyska & White 2026, arXiv:2605.28817
**"Fewer simulations, sharper covariances: Reducing mock covariance noise with Zeldovich approximation control variates"**

Brief for the 2609 journal club. Paper source: `~/Documents/projects/talks/2609_JC_covariances/papers/2605.28817/source/main.tex`.

## 1. Thesis

Sample covariance matrices from mocks converge painfully slowly — fractional error per element goes as $\sqrt{2/N_{\rm sim}}$, so percent-level covariances want $10^3$–$10^4$ mocks. This paper says: pair every mock with a Zeldovich-approximation realization run from the *same* initial conditions, and use the Zeldovich covariance — whose mean you can measure essentially noise-free, because ZA realizations are cheap — as a control variate for the mock covariance. The control-variate target here is not a mean but a *covariance matrix element*, so the objects being correlated are fourth-order in the fields. Working under a Gaussian/disconnected approximation, they get closed forms for both the optimal weight $\beta$ and the achievable correlation $\rho$, and the punchline is a very clean one: $\rho(k,k') = r^2(k)\,r^2(k')$, with $r$ the ordinary field-level cross-correlation coefficient between mock and Zeldovich fields. Since $r \to 1$ on large scales for any cosmological tracer, the covariance estimator's variance falls by $\sim 8$–$10\times$ for $k \lesssim 0.05\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, dying to nothing past $k \simeq 0.2\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ where the ZA decorrelates. Demonstrated on 10,000 masked, redshift-space, LRG-like *lognormal* mocks with a DESI SGC mask — lognormal, not N-body, because validating a covariance-of-a-covariance claim itself needs a huge ensemble.

## 2. The machinery

**Setup.** For each realization $s$, measure multipoles from the lognormal mock ($a$) and from its paired Zeldovich realization ($b$), concatenate $(k,\ell)$ bins into $d^{(s)}$ and $c^{(s)}$, and form sample covariances $X_{ij}$ (mock) and $C_{ij}$ (Zeldovich). Crucially, $\beta_{ij}$ and $\rho_{ij}$ are *scalars per covariance element*, not matrices acting on the data vector: "They are \emph{not} matrices acting on the data vector, but rather scalar coefficients for each element of the covariance matrix."

**(a) The estimator — CV on a covariance, not a mean [eq:Y].**
$$Y_{ij} = X_{ij} - \beta_{ij}\left(C_{ij} - \mu_{ij}\right)$$
Same algebra as textbook control variates, but $X$ and $C$ are themselves *second moments*. So $\mathrm{Cov}[X,C]$ is a fourth-order field statistic, and the machinery below is Wishart/Isserlis rather than simple Gaussian-mean bookkeeping. Assumption: $\mu_{ij} \equiv \mathbb{E}[C_{ij}]$ is known. Unbiasedness is then automatic *for any* $\beta$ — $\beta$ only controls how much variance you win. This is the whole safety argument of the paper: a wrong $\beta$ costs efficiency, never accuracy.

**Operationally, what is $\mu$?** Not analytic. They measure it from $N_{\rm theo}=10{,}000$ Zeldovich-only realizations *passed through the same mask*, "because the mask breaks translational invariance and induces non-trivial mode coupling in $\mu$ that is generally cumbersome to evaluate analytically." A commented-out paragraph in the source is franker still: an analytic masked $\mu$ "is not available at comparable cost/tractability." The finite-$N_{\rm theo}$ penalty is $\mathrm{Var}(C-\hat\mu)=\mathrm{Var}(C)+\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)$ with the second term $\mathcal{O}(N_{\rm th}^{-1})$ — subdominant for $N_{\rm th} \gg N_{\rm sim}$. So "known statistical properties of the Zeldovich field" means: *analytic $\beta$ and $\rho$, brute-force-but-cheap $\mu$.*

**(b) Optimal weight and the variance floor [eq:beta_opt, eq:varY_min, eq:rho].**
$$\beta_{ij}^{\rm opt} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}[X_{ij},C_{ij}]}{\mathrm{Var}[C_{ij}]}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[Y_{ij}]_{\rm min} = \mathrm{Var}[X_{ij}]\left(1-\rho_{ij}^2\right)$$
Standard least-squares projection: subtract the part of $X$ predictable from $C$. Effective simulation gain is $1/(1-\rho^2)$; $\rho=0.95$ buys a factor 10.

**(c) The Gaussian-disconnected input [eq:cov_Pk].**
$$\mathrm{Cov}\bigl[\hat P^a(k_i),\hat P^b(k_j)\bigr] \simeq \frac{2[P^{ab}(k_i)]^2}{N_{k,i}}\,\delta_{ij}$$
Assumes Gaussian fields *and* that bandpowers are uncorrelated across $k$-shells. The authors flag this is wrong in their own setup — "survey masks and window functions couple different Fourier modes and induce off-diagonal covariance between $k$-bins" — and proceed anyway, validating a posteriori.

**(d) Wishart step [eq:wishart].**
$$\mathrm{Cov}\bigl[\hat\Sigma^{aa}_{ij},\hat\Sigma^{bb}_{ij}\bigr] = \frac{1}{N_{\rm sim}-1}\left[\Sigma^{ab}_{ii}\Sigma^{ab}_{jj} + (\Sigma^{ab}_{ij})^2\right]$$
This is the "covariance of a covariance" — Isserlis–Wick on four fields. Diagonality of $\Sigma$ kills the second term off-diagonal and doubles it on the diagonal, giving a $(1+\delta_{ij})$ that then *cancels in every ratio*.

**(e) The two boxed results [eq:beta_analytic, eq:rho_analytic].** With $\alpha(k) \equiv [P^{ab}(k)]^2/[P^{bb}(k)]^2$ and $r(k) \equiv P^{ab}(k)/\sqrt{P^{aa}(k)P^{bb}(k)}$:
$$\boxed{\beta_{ij} = \alpha(k_i)\,\alpha(k_j)} \qquad \boxed{\rho_{ij} = r^2(k_i)\,r^2(k_j)}$$
Both are *independent of $N_{\rm sim}$ and of $N_k$* — they depend only on ratios of mean power spectra, which is what makes them predictable ahead of running anything. The factorization is a direct consequence of $\Sigma^{ab}_{ij} \propto \delta_{ij}$; the authors say so plainly: "Any mode coupling induced by the survey window, bin overlap, or non-Gaussian connected terms would introduce off-diagonal contributions and in general spoil this factorization."

Note the $r^2 \times r^2$ structure. At the power-spectrum level a CV wins by $r^2$; at the covariance level you pay $r^2$ twice, i.e. the covariance CV is "only about a factor of $\sim 2$ worse than what is achievable for the power spectrum itself" — much better than the naive worry that squaring the fields destroys the correlation.

**(f) Multipoles — where it stops being derived.** The Gaussian multipole covariance carries a kernel $\mathcal{K}^{ab}_{\ell\ell'}(k) = \int d\mu\,\mathcal{L}_\ell \mathcal{L}_{\ell'} [P_{ab}(k,\mu)]^2$, which is *not* diagonal in $\ell$: "in practice multipole blocks are highly correlated, so a fully non-factorized evaluation... requires the off-diagonal kernels." They punt, setting $\beta_{0,2} \approx \beta_{0,0}$ and $\beta_{2,2} \approx \beta_{0,0}$ — literally copying the monopole block. Justified only empirically, plus the argument that the covariance is dominated by the largest spectrum (the monopole, for a $b=2.2$ tracer). Legitimate because, again, a suboptimal $\beta$ cannot bias $Y$.

## 4. Headline numbers

**Variance reduction vs k** (masked, redshift space; from the diagonal-slice figures):
- k < 0.03 h/Mpc: Var[X]/Var[Y] ~ 7–8 (abstract claims "approximately an order of magnitude" for k < 0.05; the figure caption says ~7, the results text says ~8, the conclusions say ~10 — see §5).
- 0.03 < k < 0.1: ~5.
- k > 0.2: consistent with 1. rho -> 0 there, "no further variance reduction should be expected."
- There is a small dip as k -> 0, attributed to "small numerical artifact due to mask edge effects" (a source-comment exchange between the authors pins it on "some tiny mismatch between applying the mask to the Zeldovich continuous field and to the finite LRG-like sample").

**Variance reduction vs multipole:** the analytic prediction reuses the (0,0) block for (0,2) and (2,2), yet brute-force finds the (2,0) and (2,2) blocks do ~10% *better* than (0,0) on smaller scales. Unexplained.

**Effective number of mocks:** gain factor ~8 means "~100 paired simulations achieve the statistical quality that would otherwise require ~800 unpaired simulations." The abstract's version: "~10^3 paired simulations achieve the statistical precision of ~10^4 standard realizations."

**Likelihood-level number (the one to quote to a survey):** with N_data=120 (60 k-bins x 2 multipoles, 0.01 < k < 0.2), the criterion <chi2> - N_data < sqrt(N_data/2) is met at N_sim ~ 900 with CV vs N_sim ~ 1800 without. **A factor of 2, not 10.** Note they deliberately do *not* apply a Hartlap correction, because "these assumptions are not strictly satisfied in our masked, control-variate-modified covariance estimators."

**Matrix-distance accuracy:** Y closer to T than X by ~2–3x in Frobenius, roughly constant in N_sim; ~3x across the divergence metrics.

**Conditioning:** X "is not well-defined for N_sim < N_data"; Y "remains invertible and produces a stable, sensible estimate already at N_sim = N_data, indicating that CV effectively behaves like a shrinkage estimator."

**Cost of a ZA realization vs a mock:** *the paper never quantifies this.* It says only that the Zeldovich field is "cheap", "fast to generate", "computationally inexpensive". Since the targets here are lognormal mocks — themselves an FFT plus a Poisson sample, i.e. comparable in cost to a ZA realization — the paper's own setup gives no cost ratio at all. The real ratio (ZA vs N-body) is where the argument lives, and it is asserted, not measured. **This is the biggest missing number in the paper.**

**Where it does not help:** k > 0.2 h/Mpc (rho -> 0); k < 0.01 h/Mpc, which they exclude because "analytic approximations to the covariance matrix become very accurate, obviating the need for a numerical approach" (and where a Gaussian likelihood for P_l is itself dubious). So the useful window is exactly the perturbative/EFT window, 0.01 < k < 0.2.

## 5. Where the argument could be weak

**(a) The targets are lognormal, not N-body.** Their defence, in full: "Although lognormal mocks are an idealization of late-time structure formation, they reproduce the key ingredient for CV performance: the (de)correlation between the control field and the target field... in terms of the correlation coefficient r(k), the lognormal mocks have a very similar behavior to what is found for realistic DESI LRG galaxies."

My judgment: the defence is exactly right *for rho*, since rho = r^2 r^2 and r is matched by construction. It is not right for everything else. The chain rho -> variance reduction was derived under a Gaussian-disconnected assumption; a lognormal field paired with a ZA field shares a *linear* origin in a way that a nonlinear N-body field does not. Specifically, in this setup the lognormal galaxies are displaced by *the very same Zeldovich displacement field* used to build the control (§2, "displacing each galaxy from its grid position by the Zeldovich displacement field"). The mock and the control literally share the advection operator, not just the initial modes. In an N-body mock the displacement itself differs at second order and beyond. So the target-control correlation may be flattered here beyond what r(k) alone captures — r(k) is a two-point summary of a relationship that at fourth order could be much tighter in lognormal than in N-body. The paper does not test this, and it is the single strongest thing to press on.

**(b) Masking: applied, but idealized.** The mask is a binary angular selection zeroing the xy cross-section outside the DESI SGC footprint, plus a 200 Mpc/h cut from each end in z, with FKP weighting. Real: complex boundary, real holes, breaks periodicity, generates genuine mode coupling and non-diagonal covariance. Idealized: it is not a radial selection function, there is no fiber assignment, no redshift-dependent n(z), no completeness weighting. Fiber assignment is named in the intro as a thing analytic methods "struggle to capture" — and is exactly what is absent here. The mask edge effects also produce the low-k artifact the authors admit to.

**(c) Non-Gaussian connected covariance — does the CV bias it?** No, and this is the paper's real strength; it is worth being clear in the room about *why*, because it's easy to get backwards. Unbiasedness of Y follows from E[C] = mu alone: "the estimator is unbiased regardless of beta." The Gaussian-disconnected approximation enters only in the *derivation of beta and rho*, i.e. only in how much variance you win. So the connected trispectrum — super-sample covariance, the non-Gaussian small-scale terms — is *not* removed and *not* biased; it simply sits in X, sees a suboptimal beta, and is denoised less efficiently. That is the right architecture. The caveat: if the connected part is a large fraction of the covariance at some k, the actual rho falls below the analytic r^2 r^2 prediction there, and the advertised gain overstates reality. In this paper the measured gain slightly *exceeds* the analytic prediction, but that is in lognormal mocks, whose connected trispectrum is not a real one.

**(d) The one thing that *can* bias Y: mu.** The estimator's unbiasedness hinges entirely on mu being the true mean of C. mu is measured from 10,000 ZA realizations passed through the same mask — if that pipeline differs in any systematic way from the pipeline that produced the paired C (continuous field vs discrete sample, mask application, FKP normalization, TSC window), the mismatch is a *direct additive bias* on Y, not a loss of efficiency. The authors' own explanation of the low-k dip is precisely such a mismatch ("applying the mask to the Zeldovich continuous field and to the finite LRG-like sample"). It shows up here as a percent-level curiosity; in a real survey with a messier selection function it is the failure mode to worry about. The paper does not test the sensitivity of Y to mu errors.

**(e) The headline number drifts.** ~7 (figure caption), ~8 (results text), ~10 / "order of magnitude" (abstract and conclusions), while the likelihood-relevant convergence gain is a factor of 2. All are defensible readings of different quantities, but the abstract is quoting the most flattering one.

**(f) The multipole extension is asserted.** beta_02 = beta_22 = beta_00 by fiat. Safe (no bias), and empirically fine here, but they note it "should depend on how the effective multipole weights scale with the tracer bias" — for a low-bias tracer, where the quadrupole matters more, the copy-the-monopole trick has no argument behind it. Anyone applying this to ELGs or to cosmic shear should not assume it carries over.

**(g) Validation is partly self-referential.** T, the "truth", is the sample covariance of the same 10,000 lognormal mocks whose subsets give X and Y. The authors are candid that this bites: X and Y "can still disagree with T at the ~20% level" on small scales, which they attribute to T's own convergence rather than a CV bias, and show shrinks to ~1% with 5,000 mocks.

## 6. Relation to the two companion papers

**Wolz, Farah, Reischke, Alonso & Nicola, arXiv:2607.14843 — "Analytical covariances for catalogue-based pseudo-Cls".** Extends the improved Narrow Kernel Approximation (iNKA) to *catalogue-based* angular power spectra — fields sampled at discrete source positions, never pixelized, using irregularly-sampled SHTs. Their move is to split the estimator's contributions into distinct source pairs (handled by standard iNKA, each source treated as a finite "cloud") and self-pairs (computed *exactly* as effective white noise), keeping O(l_max^3) cost. Validated over 32 auto/cross combinations spanning dense noise-dominated (cosmic shear) and sparse noise-dominated (FRBs) regimes. Implemented in NaMaster.

**Nagura, Terasawa, Terawaki & Takada, arXiv:2605.16186 — cosmic shear 2PCF covariance.** Takes an iNKA harmonic covariance and Legendre-transforms it to real space, on HSC Y3 geometry. Finds it fails: "the covariance of the 2PCF obtained by using the iNKA does not reproduce the covariance measured directly from Gaussian simulations. Although the iNKA accurately models the diagonal structure of the harmonic-space covariance, residual inaccuracies in the off-diagonal components propagate through the Legendre transformation and significantly affect the real-space covariance." The weighted quartic-counts method does better.

**The structural relation.** All three papers are computing the same object — the disconnected/Gaussian covariance under a survey window — but they put it in different *load-bearing positions*:

- Wolz+ and Nagura+ use the analytic disconnected covariance as **the answer**. It goes straight into the likelihood. Every approximation in it is a potential parameter bias, so accuracy requirements are absolute: the mode-coupling kernels have to be right, including off-diagonal, at the percent level.
- Hadzhiyska & White use it as **the control**. It never appears in the final covariance. It appears in beta and rho, which govern only *efficiency*. An error in it costs simulations, not correctness.

This is a genuinely different error budget, and it is the cleanest thing to draw on a board: the same approximation is a systematic in one paper and a tuning parameter in the other.

**Would Nagura+'s off-diagonal leak matter for a control variate?** Almost certainly not as a bias, and only mildly as a gain loss — but it matters more than the naive answer suggests, and here is the distinction that matters. There are two separate places an approximation can enter H&W:

1. In *beta and rho*. Errors here are harmless in the sense that matters: a suboptimal beta cannot bias Y, only reduce the variance win. H&W already make a much cruder off-diagonal approximation than anything Nagura+ diagnose — they assume Sigma is diagonal in k, and copy the monopole block across all (l,l') — and it costs them, at most, the slight underprediction of gain visible in `variance_reduction_diag_*.pdf`. So the "residual inaccuracies in off-diagonal components" that wreck a real-space 2PCF covariance would land here as noise on an efficiency factor.
2. In *mu*. Errors here bias Y directly (see §5d). H&W dodge this entirely by measuring mu from ZA realizations rather than computing it analytically — precisely because the masked mode coupling is "cumbersome to evaluate analytically." **This is the point of contact.** If someone tried to replace the 10,000 ZA realizations with an analytic iNKA-style mu — which is the obvious cost-saving next step, and would make the method fully analytic — then Nagura+'s finding becomes directly threatening: an off-diagonal error in the analytic mu is an off-diagonal bias in the final covariance. H&W's brute-force mu is, read this way, a quiet admission that the analytic disconnected covariance is not yet trustworthy enough to be load-bearing.

**Could Wolz+'s catalogue covariance be a control here?** Not in the role of mu, at least not directly: H&W need the covariance of the *Zeldovich* field, and Wolz+ compute the covariance of a catalogue field's angular spectra. But the pairing is natural in the other direction. For a projected/angular analysis — which is where Cail lives — one would want a ZA-based control for a catalogue-based pseudo-Cl covariance, and Wolz+'s exact self-pair treatment is what makes a discretely-sampled control field's covariance computable at all. H&W explicitly gesture this way: "there is no reason why it cannot be applied to line intensity mapping surveys, imaging surveys or cosmic shear surveys with minimal changes." The honest assessment is that "minimal changes" is doing a lot of work — the derivation is in 3D k-space with a plane-parallel line of sight, and redoing it in (l, tomographic bin) space with shape noise is a real piece of work, not a substitution.

## 7. Five questions for the room

1. **The one the paper can't answer.** Your mocks are lognormal fields whose galaxies are displaced by *the same Zeldovich field* you use as the control — the target and the control share the advection operator, not merely the initial modes. In an N-body mock they share only the initial modes. r(k) is matched to DESI LRGs, but r(k) is a two-point summary and rho lives at fourth order. Do you have any reason to believe the *fourth-order* target-control relationship in lognormal mocks is representative of N-body, or could the ~8x gain be partly an artifact of the mock construction? (No test in the paper can settle this; it needs AbacusSummit.)

2. **What does a ZA realization actually cost relative to the mock it's paired with?** No number appears in the paper. For lognormal targets the ratio is plausibly near 1, in which case the method is nearly free. For N-body targets the ratio is what makes or breaks it — and the ZA realizations must also be *masked and measured*, which for 10,000 realizations at 2000 Mpc/h is not free either. What is the end-to-end wall-clock accounting for a realistic DESI application, including the N_theo = 10,000 ZA runs for mu?

3. **How sensitive is Y to an error in mu?** Unbiasedness rests entirely on mu = E[C], and you already see a low-k artifact you attribute to a mismatch between masking a continuous ZA field and masking a discrete sample. Have you propagated a deliberate perturbation in mu through to a parameter shift? In a real survey with fiber assignment and a radial selection function, "apply the same mask to the ZA field" is a much less well-defined instruction than it is here.

4. **The gain is ~8-10 per element but only ~2 in N_sim at the likelihood level (900 vs 1800). Which number should a survey plan against?** And relatedly, does the shrinkage-like behaviour at N_sim ~ N_data survive a proper accounting — you deliberately drop the Hartlap correction because its Wishart assumptions fail for Y, so what *is* the right finite-sample correction for a CV-modified precision matrix? That feels like an open problem the paper opens and does not close.

5. **Why did the (2,0) and (2,2) blocks outperform (0,0) by ~10%, given you populated their beta by copying the (0,0) block?** You decline to offer a physical reason. If the copied beta is suboptimal there yet the gain is *better*, something about the multipole covariance structure is more favourable than your monopole-dominance argument predicts — and knowing what would tell us whether this survives to a low-bias tracer, where you concede the argument may fail.

## 8. Surprising

- **rho = r^2 r^2, not r^4-something-worse.** The intuition worth carrying: going from a power-spectrum CV to a covariance CV squares the fields again, and you might expect the correlation to collapse. It doesn't — you pay r^2 twice, cleanly factorized, so the covariance CV is only ~2x worse than the P(k) CV. That "only a factor of two" is the paper's most reusable result.
- **beta and rho are independent of N_sim *and* of the number of modes per bin.** Both cancel in the ratio. So you can predict the entire performance of the method from mean power spectra, before running a single paired simulation.
- **CV acts as a shrinkage estimator.** Nowhere in the design; it falls out. Y is invertible at N_sim = N_data where X is singular. That's arguably more valuable than the variance reduction for anyone doing a many-bin joint analysis, and the paper rather buries it.
- **A noisy optimal beta is worse than a smooth approximate one.** "Using a noisy brute-force beta can degrade the estimator variance more than using a smooth approximate beta." A nice general lesson about plug-in estimators.
- **mu is brute-force, not analytic** — in a paper whose selling point is analyticity. The abstract's "known statistical properties of the Zeldovich field" means the analytic beta and rho; the actual expectation value comes from 10,000 masked ZA runs, because the masked analytic version "is not available at comparable cost/tractability" (a candid sentence the authors commented out of the submitted text).
- **The commented-out author dialogue in the source is worth reading** (`source/main.tex` around line 652 and line 683) — White pressing Hadzhiyska on why the analytic beta dips below 1 as k -> 0 when all alpha(k) should go to 1, and her attributing it to mask edge effects. It's a live, unresolved thread they chose to soften in the text.
