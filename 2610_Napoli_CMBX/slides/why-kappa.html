CMB lensing: a high-z anchor with different systematics

The CMB as a lensing source
  • one source plane, $z_* \approx 1100,$ precisely known
  • no shape noise, no intrinsic alignments, no photo-z
  • kernel peaks at $z \approx 2,$ overlapping and behind all our bins

Top: the redshift distributions of the six Euclid DR1 weak-lensing source bins, the official tomographic n(z) product (DpdBinMeanRedshift, PHZ-weighted, released May 2026), with mean redshifts from 0.45 to 1.73. Each is normalised to unit area and drawn with a light Hann smoothing of width $\Delta z = 0.05.$ The South release (WLSOUTH_R1) is drawn; the North one differs by at most 0.014 in mean redshift, invisible at this scale. Bottom: the lensing kernel $W(z)$ of each bin, the efficiency with which matter at redshift $z$ lenses that bin, in the same units for every curve so their heights compare directly. The kernels use the unsmoothed n(z), at a Planck 2018 ΛCDM background.

The second step adds the CMB lensing kernel. Its source is the last-scattering surface, a single plane at $z_* \approx 1100$ whose distance is fixed by the CMB itself, so the kernel has no redshift-distribution uncertainty. It peaks at $z \approx 1.9$ and stays large to $z = 4$ and beyond, so it overlaps every Euclid kernel and extends behind the highest bin. On the CMB side there is no shape noise, no intrinsic alignment and no photometric-redshift error: the reconstruction noise of κ is of a different kind, and independent of anything in the shear catalogue.

A cross-spectrum still inherits the galaxy side's systematics: $\gamma\kappa$ carries the sources' multiplicative bias, n(z) and an intrinsic-alignment term, and $\delta\kappa$ the galaxy bias. What the CMB adds is a second lens-plane weighting with none of those nuisances on its own side, which is what breaks the degeneracies on the next slide.
